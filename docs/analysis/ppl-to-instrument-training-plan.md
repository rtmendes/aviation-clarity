### 2.1 Hours, held to 14 CFR 61.109(a)

| Block | Dual | Solo | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| Post-solo maneuvers and pattern consolidation | 5 | 5 | |

| **Total** | **35** | **15** | Legal floor is 40 hr / 20 dual / 10 solo |

### 2.2 Realistic timing at 5+ days a week

50 hours is roughly **34 sessions** — about 25 dual at ~1.4 hr and 9 solo at ~1.7 hr. The plan
is a **minimum of five days a week, with two blocks on some days**. Northern New Jersey from
September into November still costs roughly 30% of booked slots to weather, maintenance and
instructor availability, but flying five days a week absorbs that far better than three: a
lost morning has another slot behind it rather than a three-day gap.

| Sessions/week | Pattern | Effective after 30% attrition | Weeks | Checkride-ready |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | five days, one block each | 3.5 | 9.7 | **21 Nov 2026** |
| 6 | five days, one double | 4.2 | 8.1 | **9 Nov 2026** |
| **7** | **five days, two doubles** | **4.9** | **6.9** | **1 Nov 2026** |
| 8 | five days, three doubles | 5.6 | 6.1 | 26 Oct 2026 |

Starting 14 September, that is a **late-October to late-November checkride**, not December.

### 2.3 At this cadence the examiner becomes the binding constraint, not the flying

This is the thing to fix tomorrow. Examiner lead times of 4–8 weeks are normal, and at 6–7
sessions a week the airplane finishes before the examiner is free:

| Cadence | Ready | Book by, 4-week lead | Book by, 8-week lead |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5/week | 21 Nov | 24 Oct | 26 Sep |
| 6/week | 9 Nov | 12 Oct | **14 Sep — the day training starts** |
| 7/week | 1 Nov | 4 Oct | **6 Sep — before training starts** |

**Book the DPE at enrollment**, with a date around 1–9 November and a weather alternate. If the
examiner cannot be booked that early, that fact — not the flying — sets the finish date, and
it is worth knowing before committing to the pace.

### 2.3a How to structure a double-block day

Two blocks in a day works, but not as two lessons of new material — the second one doesn't
stick. The productive shapes are:

| Phase | Morning block | Afternoon block |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-solo (roughly the first 15 dual hours) | New material — airwork, stalls, emergencies | Pattern work, a different objective, same instructor |
| Post-solo | Dual: new material or cross-country | **Solo practice consolidating the morning** |
| Cross-country phase | Dual or solo cross-country (4–5 hr block) | Ground, planning, chair-flying — not a second flight |

**First solo lands around 29 September – 6 October** at this pace, which is when double days
become genuinely efficient, because the afternoon can be solo at $196/hr instead of $282/hr.
Before that, a double day is two dual sessions with different objectives.

Two flights a day is productive; three is not. Fatigue degrades learning faster than the extra
hour buys, and it shows up as repeated lessons — the exact thing that turns 50 hours into 65.

## 3. The constraint that decides the whole instrument plan

**14 CFR 61.65(d)(1): 50 hours of cross-country time as pilot in command, of which 10 must be
in an airplane.** A 50-hour private certificate leaves you with roughly **6 hours** of it —
the solo cross-country time in 61.109(a)(5). So the instrument rating needs about **44 more
hours of cross-country PIC**, and none of it is instruction. This, not the 40 hours of
instrument time, is the expensive part of a Part 61 instrument rating.

Three regulations turn that from dead cost into useful training:

| Lever | Rule | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dual counts as PIC** | 61.51(e)(1)(i) — a private pilot logs PIC as sole manipulator of an aircraft for which they are rated, instructor aboard or not | Every instrument dual flight that lands more than 50 NM out counts as **both** instrument training and cross-country PIC |
| **Up to 20 hours in a device** | 61.65(i) and (j) — max 20 hr in an AATD (10 in a BATD), 20 hr combined | Half the 40 instrument hours can be flown at device rates instead of aircraft rates, and a device never cancels for weather |
| **Safety-pilot sharing** | 61.51(e)(1)(i) with 91.109(c) — under the hood you are sole manipulator and log PIC; your safety pilot logs SIC | Time-building hours cost roughly half each, and count as simulated instrument time too |

**Structure every instrument dual flight as a cross-country.** Twenty hours of instrument dual
flown as 3–4 hour cross-countries clears 20 of the 44 hours you owe. The required 250 NM IFR
cross-country under 61.65(d)(2)(ii) alone is worth 4–5 of them.

## 5. The Denton handoff — and a trap to avoid

### 5.1 Your instinct is right, and here is the regulation behind it

Holding the final signoff until Denton is correct, and 61.65(d)(2)(i) is why: **3 hours of
instrument flight training from an authorized instructor within 2 calendar months before the
date of the practical test.** Fly those at Denton, with a local CFII who knows the local
approaches, the local airspace and the local examiner. The practical-test endorsement under
61.39(a)(6) comes from that instructor too.

Nothing else needs to wait. Under Part 61 there is no course to complete and no school to
graduate from — you carry your logbook and your experience, and any CFII may endorse you.

### 5.2 The trap: US Aviation Academy's self-examining authority does not transfer

US Aviation Academy's Denton campus advertises **FAA-authorized self-testing authority**,
including for the Instrument Rating — genuinely valuable, because it removes DPE scheduling
delay. But it applies to graduates of *their* FAA-approved Part 141 course. To use it you
would have to enroll in that course, and **14 CFR 141.77 caps what they may credit you**:

| Where your prior instrument training was done | Maximum credit toward USAA's course |
| --- | --- |
| In another school's **Part 141 approved course** | **50%** |
| **Part 61** training, or any non-141 source | **25%** |

Either way the receiving school must test you before granting credit. Training the whole
rating at Century Air under Part 61 and then enrolling at USAA to use self-examining authority
means **repeating 75% of a Part 141 instrument course** — far more expensive than simply
hiring a DPE at Denton for the $1,000 you have already budgeted.

**Recommendation: stay Part 61 end to end, and use a DPE at Denton.** Consider USAA's course
only if you want their airline pathway, their financing, or their collegiate partnerships —
not as a shortcut to the checkride.

### 5.3 Aircraft continuity

You asked to assume Denton has the same type you train in. US Aviation Academy operates a
fleet reported at 100–130 aircraft including Cessna 152s and 172s, Senecas, King Airs and a
Tecnam P2006T, plus devices from desktop BATDs up to a Level C King Air full flight simulator.
A **Cessna 172 is the safest common denominator** — but **whether their 172s are G1000 or
analog is not published, and it decides your Century Air aircraft choice.** Ask before you
commit to the glass premium: if their 172s are steam-gauge, train instrument in Century Air's
172P/172SP and save the $760, because continuity is the whole point of the choice.

## 8. Ask before you commit

1. **Century Air's simulator: is it an FAA-approved AATD or a BATD, and what does it cost?**
   AATD means 20 creditable hours, BATD only 10 (61.65(i)). Worth thousands.
2. **Are pre- and post-flight briefings billed as ground instruction?** The difference between
   an 8-hour and a 25-hour ground line is $600–$1,500 on the private alone.
3. **Which Century Air aircraft have an IFR-approved WAAS GPS?** Required in practice for the
   "three different kinds of approaches" in 61.65(d)(2)(ii).
4. **Are US Aviation Academy's Denton Cessna 172s G1000 or analog?** This decides whether the
   glass premium buys you continuity or nothing.
5. **Denton DPE lead time and fee** — the $1,000 budget is yours, not a quote.
6. **Whether the $650 kit can be waived** and what the brochure's $150 "applicable tax" covers.
7. **Century Air's Part 61 hourly rates** — the brochure prices Part 141 packages; §3.2 of the
   companion analysis derives the rest.
8. **The 172N's rate** — same 160 hp airframe class as the 172P, not priced in the brochure.
