# Cessna 172 G1000 vs. Steam Gauges at Century Air — Cost/Benefit Analysis

**Scope:** Is the Cessna 172SP G1000 worth its price premium over Century Air's gauge
aircraft for a student working PPL → Instrument → Commercial, and can it offset training
time or checkride outcomes?

**Primary source for pricing:** Century Air *Private Pilot — Flight Training Course
Information* brochure (© 2025 Century Air Inc., footer code 1012020), pages 4–6.
**Primary source for fleet:** [Century Air — Aircraft Rental](https://centuryair.com/aircraft-rental/).
All regulatory, aircraft and avionics claims below are cited to FAA, Garmin or
manufacturer documents in [Sources](#sources).

> Verification status: pricing and fleet composition are quoted from Century Air's own
> published material. Aircraft and avionics behaviour is cited to the FAA Type Certificate
> Data Sheet and the Garmin Cockpit Reference Guide. Items that depend on the individual
> tail number (autopilot model, optional traffic/terrain/weather, whether an EGT gauge is
> fitted to a given 172N/P) are flagged as **verify with the school** — they are not assumed.

---

## 1. Bottom line

**No — not for the Private Pilot certificate.** Century Air's own price list budgets the
G1000 path at **70 hours vs. 65 hours** for the gauge 172P. The school is not claiming the
glass panel saves time; its published estimate says it takes *five hours more*. The premium
is **+$4,000 (+19.0%)** on the average-case package, and the aircraft would have to finish
the course in **57.5 hours — 7.5 hours faster than the gauge airplane** — merely to break even.

**Yes — conditionally — for the Instrument Rating and for the Commercial 10-hour
requirement**, where the G1000's PFD/MFD, GPS approach loading and autopilot are the
actual subject matter being examined, and where the airplane satisfies the 14 CFR
61.129(a)(3)(ii) technically-advanced-airplane requirement without needing the 172RG.

**There is no evidence that glass buys a better or faster checkride.** The Airman
Certification Standards are identical regardless of panel; the evaluator tests the systems
that are *installed*, so a G1000 applicant is examined on more systems, not fewer. The NTSB's
glass-cockpit safety study found a **decrease in total accident rates but an increase in
fatal accident rates** for glass aircraft, and concluded that "generalized guidance and
training are no longer sufficient" for glass avionics.

### 3.3 Break-even: how much time would glass have to save?

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| G1000 hours needed to match the 172P average package ($21,100) | **57.5 hours** — i.e. finish **7.5 hr (11.5%) faster** than the gauge student |
| G1000 hours needed to match the 172SP average package ($22,200) | **60.9 hours** — finish 4.1 hr faster |
| Cost if the G1000 student takes the *same* 65 hours as the gauge student | **$23,500** — still +$2,400 vs. 172P, +$1,300 vs. 172SP |
| The $4,000 premium, expressed in gauge dual instruction | **≈14.2 additional hours** of 172P dual at $282/hr |
| Century Air's own assumption | G1000 takes **+5 hours**, i.e. the opposite direction |

For the G1000 to pay for itself in the PPL course, it would have to reverse a 12.5-hour
swing (from the school's +5 to a required −7.5). Nothing in FAA guidance, the school's
pricing, or the NTSB study supports that.

is genuinely easier, and what it does to training time and risk. "Authority" cites the FAA
document that makes the task examinable.

### 5.1 Private Pilot (PPL)

| # | Task / control | Gauge 172N/172P | With the G1000 172SP | Easier? | Impact on training time & risk | Authority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Leaning the mixture at altitude** (your example) | Air density falls with altitude while fuel flow does not, so the mixture goes progressively richer, causing roughness and plug fouling. With a **fixed-pitch prop and no EGT**, the only feedback is rpm and engine feel: lean until rpm peaks/roughness, then enrich per POH. Crude, but it teaches the physics. | `ENGINE → LEAN` gives a **per-cylinder EGT bar graph**; `ASSIST` finds the first cylinder to peak and displays deviation from peak (ΔPEAK) in °F; fuel flow is a live number. Leaning becomes a measured, repeatable procedure. | **Yes — materially.** The single biggest genuine training gain in the PPL syllabus. | Saves *understanding* time, not flight hours. It converts a "feel" skill into a number. Risk shifts: the student may lean to a display target without knowing why. | PHAK Ch. 7 (Mixture Control, EGT); Garmin CRG "Engine Leaning" |
| 2 | **Carburetor heat** | A required control on every flight: run-up check, descents/closed throttle, and ice recognition (rpm drop then rise). Carb ice is possible below ~70 °F / >80% RH, and even at 100 °F / 50% RH. | **The control does not exist** — the IO-360 is fuel injected. Replaced by: alternate air (usually automatic), auxiliary fuel pump discipline, and hot-start technique. | Fewer controls, **different** knowledge | Removes one recurring workload item and one failure mode. Adds hot-start frustration on summer days (real, unbudgeted time). Also means a carb-ice-trained pilot who later rents a 172N/P must re-learn it. | PHAK Ch. 7 (Carburetor Icing, Fuel Injection Systems) |
| 3 | **Fuel management & endurance** | 40 gal usable, float gauges (notoriously non-linear), tank selector, and a clock. ## 6. Does it offset training time or help you pass?

**6.1 Training time.** Century Air's published estimate is **+5 hours** for the G1000 path
(70 vs. 65), not fewer. That is consistent with the NTSB's finding that
*"pilots' experience and training in conventional cockpits do not prepare them to safely
operate glass cockpit systems"* and that generalized training is no longer sufficient. Glass
adds a systems syllabus on top of the flying syllabus; at private level the flying syllabus
is what consumes the hours, and glass does not shorten it.

**6.2 Passing tests.** The FAA written and practical standards are panel-agnostic:

- The same ACS, the same tolerances, the same tasks.
- The evaluator *"is expected to test the applicant's knowledge of the systems that are
  available or installed and operative"* — so the G1000 applicant answers **more** systems
  questions, not fewer.
- The instrument ride explicitly forbids leaning on the autopilot for at least one approach
  and requires a realistic failure-mode partial-panel procedure.

There is no published FAA or school evidence that a glass panel raises first-attempt pass
rates. Anyone who claims it does should be asked for the data.

**6.3 Where the money genuinely works.**

| Path | What you pay | What you get |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **All-gauge PPL (172P), then transition** | $21,100 average | Lowest cost to certificate; strongest fundamentals (leaning by feel, carb heat, DG discipline, mental navigation) |
| **All-G1000 PPL** | $25,100 average | +$4,000 for a panel you will re-learn in the IR anyway, and 5 extra budgeted hours |
| **Hybrid: gauge PPL, glass in Stage II** | ≈ **+$380** for the last 10 hours in the G1000 (derived: 10 × ($320 − $282)) | The brochure explicitly permits transitioning to a more advanced aircraft in Stage II. You get exposure and a checkride-ready glass airplane at ~10% of the full premium |
| **G1000 for the Instrument Rating** | Premium applies to ~50 hours | This is where PFD scan, GPS approaches and autopilot modes are the actual subject matter — and where Part 141 lets you offload up to 50% into a device |
| **G1000 (TAA) for the CPL 10 hours** | 10 hours at the premium ≈ +$380 vs. 172P | Satisfies 61.129(a)(3)(ii) without the 172RG's gear/prop workload |

**Recommendation:** train the PPL in the 172P; do the last stage or two in the 172SP G1000
if you want glass exposure before the checkride (≈$380, not $4,000); commit to the G1000
(or the SR-20, if a Cirrus is your destination) for the **Instrument Rating**; use the 172RG
*or* the G1000 for the commercial TAA/complex hours depending on whether retractable-gear
time matters to your career target.

## 7. Open items to confirm with Century Air

These change the numbers and are not published:

1. The **DPE practical-test fee** (excluded from all package prices).
2. Whether a **fuel surcharge** is currently in effect at KCDW, and per which aircraft.
3. Whether renter's **insurance premiums differ** by aircraft (the $750–900 figure is generic).
4. Which **autopilot** is fitted to the 172SP NAV III — **GFC 700 or KAP 140** — since 61.129(j) TAA credit requires a two-axis autopilot integrated with nav and heading.
5. Which optional G1000 features are installed (traffic, TAWS-B, SiriusXM weather, synthetic vision) — these change both training value and the oral exam scope.
6. Whether any 172N/172P in the fleet has an **EGT gauge** fitted (changes how leaning is taught in item 5.1.1).
7. Whether the simulator centre has a **G1000-configured FTD/AATD** eligible for Part 141 credit — this is the single largest lever on instrument-course cost.
8. **Instrument and Commercial course pricing**, which is not in this brochure at all.
9. The $75 arithmetic discrepancy in the printed 172P average total.
