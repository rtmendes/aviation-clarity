## 1. Bottom line

**Fly the Cessna 172P (or the 172N, if Century Air prices it lower).** On a 50-hour Part 61
budget it is the cheapest aircraft in the fleet at **$16,365**, it is the aircraft the school
budgets no extra hours for, and it presents the smallest systems surface to the examiner.

**The G1000 costs $1,900 more (+11.6%) for the identical 50 hours** — and $3,328 more if the
school's own +5-hour assumption for glass holds. It adds an automation-management skill
element and an electronic-display-malfunction knowledge element to the checkride that the
gauge aircraft does not.

**Buy glass later, not now.** The G1000's PFD/MFD, GPS approach loading and autopilot are the
actual subject matter of the **Instrument Rating**, and the airplane satisfies the 14 CFR
61.129(a)(3)(ii) technically-advanced-airplane requirement for the **Commercial** without
needing the 172RG.

**Glass does not buy a better or faster checkride.** The Airman Certification Standards are
identical regardless of panel; the evaluator tests the systems that are *installed*, so a
G1000 applicant is examined on more systems, not fewer. The NTSB's glass-cockpit safety study
found a **decrease in total accident rates but an increase in fatal accident rates** for glass
aircraft, and concluded that "generalized guidance and training are no longer sufficient" for
glass avionics.

### 3.6 The realistic G1000 comparison

Century Air budgets **five more hours** for the glass aircraft (70 vs. 65 on its own Part 141
package pages). Applying that to the student's 50-hour plan:

| Scenario | Hours | Cost | Δ vs 172P at 50 hr |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 172P, on plan | 50 (35d/15s) | $16,365 | — |
| G1000, same hours | 50 (35d/15s) | $18,265 | **+$1,900** |
| G1000, with the school's +5 hr | 55 (39d/16s) | $19,693 | **+$3,328** |

For the G1000 to cost the same as the 172P at 50 hours it would have to finish in
**43.5 hours** (at the same 70/30 dual:solo ratio) — 6.5 hours *under* plan, against a school
estimate that says five hours *over*. That is an **11.5-hour swing** in the wrong direction,
and 43.5 hours is only 3.5 hours above the legal minimum in 61.109(a).

### 3.7 One Part 61 penalty to know about

Simulator credit is far more restrictive under Part 61 than Part 141:

| Route | Maximum device credit toward the private certificate |
| --- | --- |
| **Part 61** | **2.5 hours** (5 hours only in a Part 142 training-centre course) |
| Part 141 | 20% of the course's flight-training hours |

If a device is a big part of your plan, that alone is an argument for the Part 141 course —
it does not change which *aircraft* to pick.

## 6. Which aircraft is easiest to learn, adds no hours, and adds no checkride questions?

Scored against the three criteria that actually decide this, on a 50-hour Part 61 plan.

| Criterion | Cessna 172N / 172P | Cessna 172SP | Cessna 172SP G1000 | Cirrus SR-20 |

| **Cost, 50 hr (35d/15s)** | **$16,365** (172N not priced — ask) | $17,265 (+$900) | $18,265 (+$1,900) | $21,932 (+$5,567) |
| **Extra hours the school budgets** | **None — this is the baseline** | None (same 65-hr assumption) | **+5 hr** (70 vs 65) → +$3,328 all-in | **+15 hr** (80 vs 65) |

| **Automation to program (ACS PA.II.B.S3)** | **Effectively none** | Effectively none | **Yes** — must demonstrate programming and managing it | Yes |

| **Display-malfunction knowledge (PA.IX.C.K2d)** | Generic only | Generic only | **Aircraft-specific and examinable** | Aircraft-specific |
| **Verdict against the student's criteria** | ✅ **Best on all three** | Second — costs $900 more for no reduction in examinable scope | ✗ Fails "no extra hours" and "no extra questions" | ✗ Fails all three |

**The honest trade.** The one thing the 172P adds that the fuel-injected aircraft do not is
**carburettor heat** — plus a directional gyro that needs resetting against the compass. Both
are two-second actions, both are on the FAA knowledge test for every private applicant
regardless of what they fly, and neither is a systems item an examiner can only ask you about
because you brought that airplane. The G1000's additions are the opposite: they exist on your
checkride *only* because you chose that panel.

**Answer: the Cessna 172P** — or the 172N if the school prices it lower, since it is the same
160 hp airframe class. It is the cheapest, the slowest (an advantage for a primary student),
the one the school budgets no extra hours for, and the one that puts the fewest systems in
front of the examiner.

---

## 6A. Does glass offset training time or help you pass?

**Training time.** Century Air's published estimate is **+5 hours** for the G1000 path
(70 vs. 65), not fewer. That is consistent with the NTSB's finding that
*"pilots' experience and training in conventional cockpits do not prepare them to safely
operate glass cockpit systems"* and that generalized training is no longer sufficient. Glass
adds a systems syllabus on top of the flying syllabus; at private level the flying syllabus is
what consumes the hours, and glass does not shorten it.

**Passing tests.** The FAA written and practical standards are panel-agnostic:

- The same ACS, the same tolerances, the same tasks.
- The evaluator *"is expected to test the applicant's knowledge of the systems that are
  available or installed and operative"* — so the G1000 applicant answers **more** systems
  questions, not fewer.
- The instrument ride explicitly forbids leaning on the autopilot for at least one approach
  and requires a realistic failure-mode partial-panel procedure.

There is no published FAA or school evidence that a glass panel raises first-attempt pass
rates. Anyone who claims it does should be asked for the data.

**Where the glass money genuinely works** — all Part 61, 50-hour basis:

| Path | What you pay | What you get |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **172P for the whole PPL** | **$16,365** | Cheapest to certificate; strongest fundamentals — leaning by feel, carb heat, DG discipline, mental navigation |
| All-G1000 PPL | $18,265, or $19,693 with the school's +5 hr | +$1,900 to +$3,328 for a panel you re-learn in the instrument rating anyway |
| **Hybrid — 172P, last 10 hours in the G1000** | **≈ +$380** (10 × ($320 − $282)) | Glass exposure and a checkride-ready TAA at about a fifth of the full premium. The brochure explicitly permits transitioning to a more advanced aircraft mid-course |
| G1000 for the Instrument Rating | Premium over ~50 hr | Where PFD scan, GPS approaches and autopilot modes *are* the subject matter — and where Part 141 allows up to 50% simulator credit |
| G1000 (TAA) for the Commercial 10 hours | ≈ +$380 | Satisfies 61.129(a)(3)(ii) without the 172RG's gear and prop workload |

**Recommendation:** fly the **172P** (or 172N) for the Part 61 private certificate at
$16,365. If you want glass in your logbook before the checkride, move the last ~10 hours to
the G1000 for about $380 rather than $1,900–$3,328. Commit to the G1000 for the
**Instrument Rating**, and choose the 172RG or the G1000 for the commercial 10 hours
depending on whether retractable-gear time matters to your career target.

## 7. Open items to confirm with Century Air

These change the numbers and are not published:

1. **The Cessna 172N's rate** — it is in the fleet but absent from the brochure, and it is the same 160 hp airframe class as the recommended 172P.
2. **The solo (aircraft-only) rate for the 172SP, G1000 and SR-20** — §3.2 derives these as dual minus the $86/hr instructor charge, a model that reproduces the printed 172P solo rate exactly, but they are not published.
3. **Part 61 pricing generally** — the brochure prices Part 141 packages, so confirm the school bills Part 61 training at the same hourly rates.
4. The **DPE practical-test fee** (excluded from all package prices).
5. Whether a **fuel surcharge** is currently in effect at KCDW, and per which aircraft.
6. Whether renter's **insurance premiums differ** by aircraft (the $750–900 figure is generic).
7. Which **autopilot** is fitted to the 172SP NAV III — **GFC 700 or KAP 140** — since 61.129(j) TAA credit requires a two-axis autopilot integrated with nav and heading.
8. Which optional G1000 features are installed (traffic, TAWS-B, SiriusXM weather, synthetic vision) — these change both training value and the oral exam scope.
9. Whether any 172N/172P in the fleet has an **EGT gauge** fitted (changes how leaning is taught in item 5.1.1).
10. Whether the simulator centre has a **G1000-configured FTD/AATD** eligible for Part 141 credit — this is the single largest lever on instrument-course cost.
11. **Instrument and Commercial course pricing**, which is not in this brochure at all.
12. The $75 arithmetic discrepancy in the printed 172P average total.
